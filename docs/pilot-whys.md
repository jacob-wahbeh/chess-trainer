## Nimzowitsch Defense: Kennedy Variation

*Open the card:* http://localhost:5174/drill?card=draco_jaco%7Cwhite%7C4886445385944472%7Cc2c3

### Take on e5 and keep c3 for your knight

**The opening's ideas**

- **Settle the d4 tension** (Both): After 2...e5, Black's pawn and knight both attack d4, so 3...Nxd4 wins a pawn unless you act. Theory gives three answers: push with 3.d5, take on e5, or defend with 3.Nf3. It calls the push and the capture the most testing.
- **Make the knight pay** (You): Black brought the knight out before the e-pawn, so the best replies make it move again. After 3.d5 Nce7 4.Nf3 it must go on to g6 to guard e5. After 3.dxe5 Nxe5 it is hit by 4.Nf3, or by 5.f4 after 4.Nc3 Bc5. Tartakower condemned 2...e5 because of the capture on e5 followed by f4.
- **Keep c3 for your knight** (You): Your queen's knight belongs on c3, where it guards e4 and d5. Put a pawn there, as in the Ponziani Opening, and e4 is left loose and a black queen on d5 cannot be chased. In related lines Black even spends a check just to provoke c3.
- **Black's ...d5 break** (Opponent): Against slow set-ups Black frees the game with ...d5, striking at e4 and opening lines for Black's pieces. Allow it cheaply, as after 3.c3 d5 or in the Scotch line after 3.Nf3, and the game is level.

**Your habit 3.c3.** 3.c3 holds d4 but lets Black strike at once with 3...d5, hitting your e4 pawn, which no piece defends. After 4.dxe5 Be6 5.exd5 Qxd5 Black's queen faces yours on the open d-file, and you cannot chase it with Nc3 because your own pawn stands on c3. After 6.Nf3 Qxd1+ 7.Kxd1 O-O-O+ you are a pawn up for now, but your king has lost the right to castle and stands in check in the middle while Black has more pieces out, and the position is level.

*Strays from:* It works against 'Keep c3 for your knight': the pawn takes the knight's square, so e4 has no defender against 3...d5 and nothing can chase Black's queen from d5.

**The taught move 3.dxe5.** 3.dxe5 answers the double attack by capturing first, and after 3...Nxe5 Black's knight stands in the centre with nothing defending it. 4.Nc3 develops onto the square 3.c3 would have taken, guarding e4 and d5. After 4...Bc5 5.f4 Nc6 the knight is back on c6 after three moves, while your pawns on e4 and f4 hold the centre. GM Nigel Davies rates 5.f4 here as promising for White, and masters choose 3.dxe5 in 43% of games.

*Carries out:* It carries out 'Make the knight pay': 3...Nxe5 brings the knight to a square where your pieces and pawns can hit it, and 5.f4 sends it back to c6 while you gain space.

> **Chase undefended pieces with pawns**: A knight in the centre with no pawn to protect it is a target for your pawns: after 3.dxe5 Nxe5 4.Nc3 Bc5, 5.f4 gains space and sends it back to c6, while 3.c3 spends a pawn move that attacks nothing.

**Other moves you might try**

- **3.d5** (sound; idea: Make the knight pay)
  - *Right after you play it:* 3.d5 steps out of the double attack, gains space and drives Black's knight to a passive square. After 3...Nce7 4.Nf3 Ng6 the knight has used three moves to reach g6, and 5.h4 threatens to push on and kick it again. 5...h5 stops that but gives up control of g5, which 6.Bg5 uses at once.
  - *Once you've found the taught move:* Both moves keep your edge equally well, and the app accepts 3.d5. The difference is the kind of game. 3.d5 locks the centre and you play with extra space on the wings, as 5.h4 does here. 3.dxe5 keeps the game open and you play with quick development and pawn moves that hit the knight, as in 4.Nc3 Bc5 5.f4. Masters choose 3.dxe5 in 43% of games and 3.d5 in 31%. Club players pick 3.d5 most, in 65% of games.
- **3.Nf3** (inaccuracy; idea: Black's ...d5 break)
  - *Right after you play it:* 3.Nf3 develops and guards d4, but after 3...exd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nxc6 bxc6 6.Bd3 d5 7.exd5 cxd5 your knight has moved three times only to be swapped off. Black's 6...d5 leaves Black the only pawn in the centre, and the position is level. Masters still choose it in 25% of games: it steers a 1...Nc6 player into a sound main-line Scotch Game, and IM Andrew Martin left 2...e5 out of a 1...Nc6 repertoire course rather than allow it. The engine's label only means 3.Nf3 hands back the small extra edge that 2...e5 allowed.
  - *Once you've found the taught move:* 3.dxe5 makes the exchange on your terms. After 3...Nxe5 it is Black's knight that gets chased, as in 4.Nc3 Bc5 5.f4 Nc6. You keep the only pawn in the centre on e4, and your knight on c3 guards d5, so Black has no easy ...d5. That keeps the small edge 3.Nf3 hands back.

## Sicilian Defense: Alapin Variation

*Open the card:* http://localhost:5174/drill?card=draco_jaco%7Cblack%7C6104332673881535%7Cd7d6

### Against 2.c3, attack e4 with 2...Nf6 while White can't play Nc3

**The opening's ideas**

- **White wants two centre pawns** (Opponent): 2.c3 prepares 3.d4, so that after ...cxd4 White can recapture with the c-pawn and keep pawns on both d4 and e4. That undoes the usual Sicilian deal, where Black swaps the c-pawn for White's d-pawn and ends up with more centre pawns.
- **No Nc3 for White** (You): The price of 2.c3 is that the pawn takes the knight's best square: White can't guard e4 with Nc3, and can't use Nc3 to chase your pieces from d5. Black's main replies are built to exploit exactly this.
- **Strike the centre at once** (You): Sveshnikov, the leading 2.c3 specialist, says Black has essentially two good replies, and both attack e4 at once, so White can't follow up with 3.d4 in peace. Moves that work against 2.Nf3, such as ...d6, are too slow here and let White build the full centre for free.
- **Chip at the e5 pawn** (Both): After 3.e5 Nd5, White's pawn has gained space and driven the knight from f6, but it is now a target: Black trades on d4 with ...cxd4 and then hits the pawn with ...d6. Wikipedia compares this to Alekhine's Defence with c3 and ...c5 added, extra moves that help Black.

**Your habit 2...d6.** 2...d6 attacks neither e4 nor d4, so White plays 3.d4 and takes the full centre for free. Your 3...Nf6 then comes a move late: White simply guards e4 with the developing 4.Bd3, and after 4...cxd4 5.cxd4 White has pawns on d4 and e4, with the c3 square free for a knight again. Even after 5...e5 claims a share of the centre, 6.Nf3 Be7 7.O-O leaves White castled with a small but lasting space edge.

*Strays from:* It neglects 'Strike the centre at once': 2...d6 makes no threat, so 2.c3 gets exactly what it was played for, two centre pawns after 3.d4.

**The taught move 2...Nf6.** 2...Nf6 develops and attacks e4, and with a pawn on c3 White has no Nc3 to defend it, so White usually pushes 3.e5. The knight goes to 3...Nd5, safe for the same reason (on 3...Ne4, 4.d3 traps it), and when 4.d4 threatens 5.c4 and 6.d5 to chase it, 4...cxd4 5.cxd4 pulls White's c-pawn to d4. Then 5...d6 hits the e5 pawn, and after 6.Nf3 Nc6 7.Bc4 the chances are equal.

*Carries out:* It carries out 'Strike the centre at once': because no knight can come to c3 to guard e4, White has to answer the threat, usually with 3.e5, instead of calmly building pawns on d4 and e4.

> **Fight for the centre**: Contest the centre your opponent is building: 2.c3 prepares 3.d4, so attack e4 with 2...Nf6 now instead of letting White set up pawns on both d4 and e4.

**Other moves you might try**

- **2...Nc6** (slightly worse; idea: White wants two centre pawns)
  - *Right after you play it:* 2...Nc6 develops, but White simply plays 3.d4, the move 2.c3 was there to support. After the natural 3...cxd4 4.cxd4 White has the two-pawn centre, and Wikibooks rates even the main follow-up 4...d5 as slightly better for White. The engine prefers 3...d5, and after 4.exd5 Qxd5 you reach the same position as 2...d5 3.exd5 Qxd5 4.d4 Nc6, so the damage is small; it is the top club move here (35% of games) but rare among masters.
  - *Once you've found the taught move:* 2...Nf6 gives White no free 3.d4: it attacks e4, and with no Nc3 available White usually pushes 3.e5. After 3...Nd5 4.d4 cxd4 5.cxd4 d6 you are already attacking White's centre instead of watching it being built.
- **2...e6** (slightly worse; idea: Strike the centre at once)
  - *Right after you play it:* 2...e6 is solid and prepares ...d5, but it gives White time for 3.d4, and after 3...d5 4.e5 you are in an Advance French. White's e5 pawn gains space, and your light-squared bishop is shut in behind the e6 pawn, getting only as far as d7 in 4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bd7 6.Be2 Nge7 7.Na3. Masters play it in about 8% of games and the engine rates it only a little below the main moves, so it suits French players, but most Sicilian players don't want this structure.
  - *Once you've found the taught move:* 2...Nf6 keeps the game a Sicilian: after 3.e5 Nd5 4.d4 cxd4 5.cxd4 d6 the e5 pawn is under attack at once, and your c8-bishop's diagonal is open instead of blocked by a pawn on e6.
- **2...d5** (sound; idea: No Nc3 for White)
  - *Right after you play it:* 2...d5 hits back in the centre at once and is the old main line, played in a third of master games. After 3.exd5 Qxd5 your queen stands well in the centre, because the c3 pawn stops White from hitting it with Nc3, and 4.d4 no longer gives White two centre pawns, while you lead in development. Trades on d4, as in 6.Be2 cxd4 7.cxd4, usually leave White with an isolated d-pawn.
  - *Once you've found the taught move:* Both are main lines, but 2...Nf6 is more popular with masters (44.6% to 33.2%) and the engine rates it fully level, while 2...d5 leaves White a very small edge. 2...d5 opens the centre at once, so you must be ready for isolated-pawn middlegames or for a queenless ending such as 4.d4 Nc6 5.Nf3 Nf6 6.dxc5 Qxd1+ 7.Kxd1, where White's queenside pawns are the easier ones to push. 2...Nf6 instead invites 3.e5 and then attacks that pawn with ...d6.

## Italian Game: Evans Gambit, Pierce Defense

*Open the card:* http://localhost:5174/drill?card=draco_jaco%7Cwhite%7C15856967415293%7Cf3g5

### Castle to break the pin on c3, then retake with 8.cxd4

**The opening's ideas**

- **Unpin, then rebuild the centre** (You): Black's a5-bishop pins your c3-pawn to the king on e1, so you cannot take back on d4 with the c-pawn yet. Once your king leaves e1 the pin is gone, and 8.cxd4 restores the e4/d4 pawn centre you gave the b-pawn for.
- **Development is your compensation** (You): You are two pawns down, and you are paid in time: a lead in development and open lines while Black's king is still in the centre. That pay disappears if you spend moves on pieces that are already out, and your b1-knight and a1-rook are the slowest to get into play, because the knight's natural c3 square is still blocked by your own pawn.
- **Hit f7 with tempo** (You): Your queen on b3 and bishop on c4 form a battery against f7, so Black has to spend a move defending it, as with 7...Qf6. A knight jump to g5 belongs later, after Black's king knight has gone to e7 and can no longer guard f7 from h6.
- **Greed needs a spare tempo** (Both): If Black takes a third pawn on c3 straight after you castle, the line is called the Compromised Defence and is poorly regarded, because your pieces come out with gain of time and you get a very dangerous attack. After a wasted move like 7.Ng5, Black can first cover f7 with 7...Nh6, and then the same grab, 8...dxc3, is safe.

**Your habit 7.Ng5.** 7.Ng5 moves the f3-knight a second time to hit f7 while your b1-knight and c1-bishop are still at home, and 7...Nh6 covers f7 with a developing move. Your c3-pawn is still pinned, so you castle a move late with 8.O-O and Black takes a third pawn with 8...dxc3. After 9.Qb3 O-O 10.Nxc3 d6 11.h3 Ne5 Black is castled with f7 guarded by the rook and the h6-knight, the knight on e5 hits your c4-bishop, and you are two pawns down with no centre and no lead in development left.

*Strays from:* It neglects 'Unpin, then rebuild the centre': the move goes into a second knight trip while the pin on c3 stays, so Black gets time to guard f7 and grab c3 before you can retake on d4.

**The taught move 7.O-O.** Castling takes your king off e1, so the a5-bishop no longer pins the c3-pawn and 8.cxd4 is threatened at once. In the engine's line 7...Nf6 8.cxd4 Nxe4 Black takes the e4-pawn and is two pawns up again, but you gain time with every move: 9.d5 hits the c6-knight, 10.Qd4 forks the loose e4-knight and the g7-pawn, and 11.Bg5 brings out another piece while Black's king is still in the centre. It is the top master choice (58.7%), and the position stays roughly level despite the two pawns.

*Carries out:* It carries out 'Unpin, then rebuild the centre': with your king off e1, 8.cxd4 puts a pawn back on d4 beside the e4-pawn.

> **Deal with pins early**: Your c3-pawn is pinned to your king, so deal with the pin quickly: 7.O-O breaks it and makes 8.cxd4 possible at once, while 7.Ng5 leaves it in place and gives Black time for 7...Nh6 and 8...dxc3.

**Other moves you might try**

- **7.Qb3** (sound; idea: Hit f7 with tempo)
  - *Right after you play it:* Good move. Your queen joins the c4-bishop against f7, so Black has to spend the reply on defence, as with 7...Qf6, instead of developing a minor piece. Masters play it in 41.3% of games and the engine rates it among the best moves here.
  - *Once you've found the taught move:* The two moves are equally good: masters castle in 58.7% of games against 41.3% for 7.Qb3, while the engine slightly prefers 7.Qb3. The difference is the order: 7.Qb3 makes Black defend f7 first (7...Qf6) and breaks the pin a move later with 8.O-O, while 7.O-O breaks it at once and keeps your queen free for moves like 10.Qd4. After 7.Qb3 Qf6 8.O-O d3 9.e5 Qg6 you reach the position of Anderssen's Evergreen Game (1852), where GM Matthew Sadler prefers 10.Rd1, the engine's move, which wins back the d3-pawn after 10...Nge7 11.Bxd3.
- **7.Nxd4** (mistake; idea: Unpin, then rebuild the centre)
  - *Right after you play it:* This wins one pawn back, and that is all it does. After 7...Nge7 Black develops calmly and stays a clean pawn up, and your e4-pawn stands alone in the centre with nothing to show for the gambit. Club players choose it in 12.7% of games; masters do not play it.
  - *Once you've found the taught move:* 7.O-O keeps the recapture for the c-pawn: once your king leaves e1 the pin is gone, and after 7...Nf6 8.cxd4 you have pawns on e4 and d4 again, ready to gain time with 9.d5. GM Mihail Marin calls 7.Nxd4 illogical because it gives up that pawn centre, and Wikipedia notes it sets Black few problems.
